## Motivation / Background

I spent the last 1.5 years at Amazon Robotics on the team responsible for robotic simulation. As an SDE, I worked across the Omniverse stack, USD, and building simulated environments in software like Unity. Naturally when working in “Big Tech”, I worked on specific parts of the pipeline; the RL training and tinkering was left to the Applied Scientists.

**Goal:** See the end-to-end flow of preparing assets, choosing a simulated engine, tinkering with an RL policy, and finally examining the Sim2Sim gap. And escape Amazon tooling to get a better idea of industry standards

## Preamble

At Amazon, we had a managed way to provision GPU-enabled cloud instances immediately equipped with simulated environments (A tool I helped build!). But we are operating outside the Amazon “ecosystem” and will need to do things manually.

I chose AWS because I’m most familiar with it, using a g6e.2xlarge type instance.

After gaining a quota from AWS for g6e type instances and wrestling with DCV and some driver mismatches in the base Isaac Sim AMI, I’m in with a working cartpole trained on PhysX (basically the “Hello World” of RL training in Isaac Sim)

![Cartpole trained on PhysX in Isaac Lab](cartpole-physx.gif)

## Plan

Train a PPO model to bounce a ball on a paddle as many times as possible in Isaac Lab. Then, port the policy to MuJoCo-Warp and Kamino and observe the Sim2Sim gap. Then, train a model in MuJoCo-Warp and observe it in Isaac Lab (PhysX).

Isaac Lab 3.0 can run the same environment on PhysX, MuJoCo-Warp, and Kamino with one setting which is great convenience for this experiment.

![Isaac Lab 3.0: one environment, three physics engines](pipeline.svg)

Observing the Sim2Sim gap will thus be observing gaps from the conversion pipeline and the physics between both engines.

## Assets

The rig was written in MJCF (MuJoco’s format), Isaac Lab converts it to a layered Universal Scene Descriptor (USD) file.

Our assets are simple and generated by LLM. We just have a paddle, three joints that control the height, roll, and pitch of the paddle, and a ball.

## Building the RL model

I opted to use AI for most of the scaffolding.

The action space is the set of all possible joint positions and we define the relative center of each run as the center of the paddle’s at its home (starting) position.

Our reward function to start will be simple, +1 per bounce, -10 for dropping the ball. The episode timeout after 20 seconds.[^1]

The first iteration led to the paddle wildly bouncing the ball, it averaged about 1 bounce per episode (the 1 free starting bounce), for a reward of about -9.

I’ll gloss over the tuning as that isn’t the focus here. I eventually settled on immediate penalties when the ball leaves the column space above the paddle, along with reducing exploration noise from 1.0 -> 0.3 to discourage the paddle from shaking wildly. This resulted in the paddle learning to just hold the ball, bouncing it incredibly lightly.

![The paddle holding the ball, bouncing it incredibly lightly](chattering.png)

Finally, after redefining a bounce as the ball leaving the paddle fast enough to rise at least 5cm, our policy finally began to exhibit desired behavior

![The trained policy bouncing the ball (PhysX)](physx-policy-on-physx.gif)

## The engine swap

As mentioned before, swapping the engines risks changes in physics and data from our original assets being lost.

This is how our PhysX policy looks in MuJoCo-Warp

![The PhysX-trained policy running on MuJoCo-Warp](physx-policy-on-mjwarp.gif)

| Engine | Bounces per episode | Survival |
|---|---|---|
| PhysX | 102 | 97.7% |
| MuJoCo-Warp | 55 | 100% |
| Kamino | 33 | 100% |

The issue is the bounciness: we added an inherent bounciness value to the ball that was lost in the solvers, not the conversion. The policy still knows to ‘lift’ a ball up, which allows it to maintain some bouncing.

Investigation showed that the model preserved the bounciness, but MuJoCo-Warp can’t express restitution.

## Training in the reverse

We used the same parameters to train a policy in MuJoCo-Warp then port it back to Isaac Lab.

![The MuJoCo-trained policy running in Isaac Lab (PhysX)](mjwarp-policy-on-physx.gif)

This revealed that the loss is only one-directional: the MuJoCo-trained policy failed almost immediately in Isaac Lab, while the PhysX-trained policy held up in MuJoCo-Warp.

[^1]: A bounce is counted when the ball's vertical velocity flips from downward to upward. Dropping the ball means the ball's center falls below the paddle's center; the episode ends there with the −10.
