## Motivation / Background

I spent the last 1.5 years at Amazon Robotics on the team responsible for robotic simulation. As an SDE, I worked across the Omniverse stack, USD, and building simulated environments in software like Unity. Naturally when working in “Big Tech”, I worked on specific, small parts of the pipeline.

**Goal:** See the end-to-end flow of preparing assets, choosing a physics engine, tinkering with an RL policy, and finally examining the Sim2Sim gap. And escape Amazon tooling to get a better idea of industry standards

## Plan

We will train a PPO policy to bounce a ball on a paddle as many times as possible in Isaac Lab. Then, staying in Isaac Lab, we'll swap the physics engine underneath that same policy to MuJoCo-Warp and Kamino to observe the Sim2Sim gap. Finally, we'll train a policy on MuJoCo-Warp and see how it holds up on PhysX.

Isaac Lab 3.0 can run the same environment on PhysX, MuJoCo-Warp, and Kamino with one setting which is great convenience for this experiment.

![Isaac Lab 3.0: one environment, three physics engines](pipeline.svg)

Observing the Sim2Sim gap will thus be observing gaps from the conversion pipeline and the physics between the engines.

## Assets

The rig was written in MJCF (MuJoCo’s format), Isaac Lab converts it to a layered Universal Scene Descriptor (USD) file.

Our assets are simple and generated by LLM. We just have a paddle, three joints that control the height, roll, and pitch of the paddle, and a ball.

## Building the RL model

I opted to use AI for most of the scaffolding.

The action space is the set of all possible joint positions and we define the relative center of each run as the center of the paddle’s at its home (starting) position.

Our reward function to start will be simple, +1 per bounce, -10 for dropping the ball. The episode timeout after 20 seconds.[^1]

The first iteration led to the paddle wildly bouncing the ball, it averaged about 1 bounce per episode (the 1 free starting bounce), for a reward of about -9.

I’ll gloss over the tuning as that isn’t the focus here. I eventually settled on immediate penalties when the ball leaves the column space above the paddle, along with reducing exploration noise from 1.0 -> 0.3 to discourage the paddle from shaking wildly. This resulted in the paddle learning to just hold the ball, bouncing it incredibly lightly. This was textbook reward hacking: my bounce rule only checked that the ball's vertical velocity flipped from down to up, so every tiny jitter of the ball on a vibrating paddle counted as a "bounce". The policy racked up hundreds of them per episode without ever really bouncing the ball.

![The paddle holding the ball, bouncing it incredibly lightly](chattering.png)

Finally, after redefining a bounce as the ball leaving the paddle fast enough to rise at least 5cm, our policy began to exhibit desired behavior.

![The trained policy bouncing the ball (PhysX)](physx-policy-on-physx.gif)

## The engine swap

As mentioned before, swapping the physics engine risks changes in the simulation's physics math, and data from our original assets being lost.

This is how our PhysX policy looks in MuJoCo-Warp

![The PhysX-trained policy running on MuJoCo-Warp](physx-policy-on-mjwarp.gif)

| Engine | Bounces per episode | Survival |
|---|---|---|
| PhysX | 102 | 97.7% |
| MuJoCo-Warp | 55 | 100% |
| Kamino | 33 | 100% |

The issue is the bounciness: we added an inherent bounciness value to the ball that was lost in the solvers, not the conversion. The policy still knows to ‘lift’ a ball up, which allows it to maintain some bouncing.

Investigation showed that the model preserved the bounciness, but MuJoCo-Warp can’t express restitution.

## Training in the reverse

We used the same parameters to train a policy on MuJoCo-Warp, then switched the physics engine back to PhysX to test it.

![The MuJoCo-Warp-trained policy running on PhysX](mjwarp-policy-on-physx.gif)

| Engine | Bounces per episode | Survival |
|---|---|---|
| MuJoCo-Warp | 90 | 99.6% |
| Kamino | 45 | 100% |
| PhysX | 1.2 | 1.6% |

This revealed that the transfer is asymmetric: the PhysX-trained policy held up on MuJoCo-Warp (fewer bounces, but it never dropped the ball), while the MuJoCo-Warp-trained policy failed almost immediately on PhysX.

[^1]: A bounce is counted when the ball's vertical velocity flips from downward to upward. Dropping the ball means the ball's center falls below the paddle's center; the episode ends there with the −10.
